Which physical theories of consciousness survive the quantitative tests of decoherence, locality, and quantum information?

8.1 Decoherence bounds in biological matter

8.1.1 Timescales, thermal noise, and where quantum effects actually survive

Many proposals claim that consciousness relies on brain-wide quantum entanglement or macroscopic superpositions inside warm neurons. Before accepting any such claim, we must calculate the decoherence timescale \(\tau_D\). In a warm, wet biological environment at \(T \approx 310\,\text{K}\), ions, water molecules, and electric fields scatter continuously off any superposed charge distribution.

Using standard Caldeira–Leggett / collisional decoherence estimates for a spatial superposition of separation \(\Delta x\), the coherence decays exponentially as \(e^{-t/\tau_D}\). For a single hydrated ion or microtubule dipole displaced across nanometer scales in water, Tegmark’s calculation gives

\[\tau_D \sim 10^{-20}\text{ to }10^{-13}\,\text{s},\]

whereas neural action potentials and cognitive binding unfold over \(\tau_{\text{neural}} \sim 10^{-3}\text{ to }10^{-1}\,\text{s}\)—a gap of ten to seventeen orders of magnitude!

Does this mean quantum mechanics is irrelevant to biology? Not at all. Quantum mechanics governs chemical bonds, enzyme tunneling, and spin-dependent radical pairs in magnetoreception (which can last microseconds inside protected protein pockets). What decoherence does rule out is unshielded, brain-wide quantum computation operating in spatial superpositions at room temperature without active quantum error correction.

Worked check 8.1. Why can an electron-spin radical pair inside a cryptochrome protein remain coherent for microseconds while a superposed sodium ion across a neuron membrane decoheres in femtoseconds?

Reasoned solution

A neutral spin pair couples weakly to fluctuating electric fields and sits shielded inside a protein pocket, whereas a charged ion moving nanometers couples strongly via long-range Coulomb scattering to surrounding water dipoles.

8.2 Objective collapse proposals and empirical limits

8.2.1 Orch-OR, localization rates, and experimental bounds

Penrose and Hameroff’s Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch-OR) proposes that consciousness occurs when gravitationally induced wavefunction collapse terminates coherent superpositions in neuronal microtubules. Unlike standard decoherence, objective-collapse models (such as Diósi–Penrose or Continuous Spontaneous Localization, CSL) modify the Schrödinger equation with stochastic terms.

That modification makes crisp, testable predictions: spontaneous localization imparts tiny random kicks to charged particles, producing anomalous heating and X-ray/gamma-ray emission even in cold, isolated crystals. Underground high-purity germanium detector experiments (such as Gran Sasso tests) have strongly constrained the simplest Diósi–Penrose parameter regimes, pushing the required localization scale far beyond bare nuclear radii. Any viable theory of consciousness should therefore avoid depending on unverified modifications of quantum dynamics unless direct experiments confirm them.

Worked check 8.2. Why do underground X-ray detectors test gravitational collapse proposals without ever looking at a brain?

Reasoned solution

If a universal collapse law depends only on mass distribution, it must act on every crystal on Earth as well as on microtubules. Random localization kicks accelerate atomic nuclei slightly, emitting characteristic radiation that underground detectors can bound directly.

8.3 Quantum Integrated Information and global workspaces

8.3.1 Non-factorizability across cuts versus classical integration

If consciousness does not require exotic collapse or brain-wide room-temperature entanglement, how do leading neuroscientific frameworks—Global Workspace Theory (GWT) and Integrated Information Theory (IIT)—fit inside quantum mechanics?

Both generalize naturally to open quantum systems. In Quantum Integrated Information (Zanardi, Tomka, and Venuti; Kleiner and Tull), a subsystem \(A\) with state \(\rho\) and quantum channel \(\mathcal{E}\) is partitioned across bipartitions \(A = A_1 \mid A_2\). One measures how much information or causal structure is lost under the “cut” channel \(\mathcal{E}_{A_1 \mid A_2}\) that severs cross-partition interactions:

\[\Phi(\rho, \mathcal{E}) = \min_{A_1 \mid A_2} D\bigl(\mathcal{E}(\rho) \,\|\, \mathcal{E}_{A_1 \mid A_2}(\rho)\bigr),\]

where \(D\) is a distinguishability measure such as quantum relative entropy or trace distance. Notice two decisive advantages of this formulation: first, it works seamlessly both when \(\rho\) is a classical mixture of decohered pointer states (as in a biological brain) and when \(\rho\) carries genuine quantum entanglement (as in a fault-tolerant quantum processor); second, it respects unitarity, decoherence, and no-signalling automatically.

Worked check 8.3. If a composite channel \(\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{E}_1 \otimes \mathcal{E}_2\) acts independently on \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) starting from a product state \(\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2\), what is \(\Phi(\rho, \mathcal{E})\)?

Reasoned solution

Cutting the partition \(A_1 \mid A_2\) changes nothing because no cross-partition interaction exists. Thus \(\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \mathcal{E}_{A_1 \mid A_2}(\rho)\) and \(\Phi = 0\).
