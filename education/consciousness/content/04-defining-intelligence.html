How can we define intelligence purely in terms of open physical systems, information, and control?

4.1 The agent–environment cut

4.1.1 Boundaries, sensors, actuators, and internal states

Every physical description of an intelligent system begins by partitioning a larger Hilbert space into an agent \(A\) and an environment \(E\):

\[\mathcal{H}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_E.\]

Within \(\mathcal{H}_A\), the agent’s degrees of freedom decompose functionally into sensory registers \(S_A\) (which couple to incoming environmental signals), an internal memory and model register \(M_A\) (which persists across cycles), and actuator registers \(C_A\) (which exert forces or apply feedback operations back onto \(E\)).

What distinguishes an agent from an ordinary rock sitting in a stream? Both sit inside \(\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_E\), and both exchange energy and correlations with \(E\). A rock, however, responds passively to local forces without maintaining a protected internal state that guides systematic, goal-directed counter-actions. An agent maintains a Markov-like or decoherence-stabilized boundary: its sensory channels map environmental regularities into \(M_A\), and its internal state selects actuator responses that steer future environmental trajectories toward preferred target regions.

Worked check 4.1. Why does a passive thermal conductor (like a copper rod between hot and cold baths) fail to qualify as an intelligent agent?

Reasoned solution

Although it exchanges energy across a boundary, a copper rod does not encode compressed representations of varying external states into an internal memory \(M_A\) or select conditional feedback actions to optimize an objective.

4.2 World-models, prediction, and control

4.2.1 Minimizing surprise and maximizing goal achievement

Suppose at discrete time steps \(t = 1, 2, \dots\), an agent receives sensory outcomes \(o_t\), updates its internal model state \(\sigma_t \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_M)\), and chooses an action \(a_t\) drawn from a policy \(\pi(a_t \mid \sigma_t)\). Two complementary mathematical criteria govern how well the agent functions:

  1. Predictive fidelity (model quality): Given past observations \(o_{1:t}\), the agent’s model induces a predictive distribution \(q(o_{t+1} \mid \sigma_t, a_t)\). Against the true conditional environment distribution \(p(o_{t+1} \mid o_{1:t}, a_{1:t})\), the agent’s expected prediction error is measured by the Kullback–Leibler divergence \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| q)\). Lowering \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) means the internal state \(\sigma_t\) captures genuine causal structure in \(E\).
  2. Goal achievement (control utility): For a task specified by a utility or reward function \(R(s_t, a_t)\) (or homeostatic target subspace \(\mathcal{H}_{\text{goal}} \subset \mathcal{H}_{AE}\)), the agent maximizes expected cumulative return \(\mathbb{E}_\pi\bigl[\sum_t \gamma^t R_t\bigr]\) while paying the thermodynamic work cost \(W_t \ge k_B T \, \Delta S_t\) of sensing, updating, and erasing memory.

Notice the intimate link between prediction and control: an agent with a poor predictive model cannot reliably steer \(E\) toward \(\mathcal{H}_{\text{goal}}\) across unfamiliar perturbations, whereas a pure lookup table that memorizes fixed stimulus-response pairs collapses as soon as the environment shifts.

Worked check 4.2. Consider a single-qubit phase-stabilization loop where drift rotates \(\ket{+}\) by an unknown angle \(\delta\theta\) each step. Why must the controller estimate \(\delta\theta\) (build a model) rather than apply a fixed corrective rotation?

Reasoned solution

A fixed rotation only cancels one specific drift value. When \(\delta\theta\) varies over time, only an agent that updates an internal estimate \(\hat{\theta}_t\) from measurement outcomes can adaptively apply \(R_z(-\hat{\theta}_t)\) to keep fidelity high.

4.3 A formal physical definition of intelligence

4.3.1 Bounded adaptive compression and feedback control

We are now ready to state our first central definition—one that uses zero vague mental terms and applies equally to biological organisms, classical robots, and quantum processors:

Definition (Physical Intelligence). An open physical system \(A\) coupled to an environment \(E\) exhibits intelligence with respect to a class of environments \(\mathcal{E}\) and an objective \(R\) if, subject to finite memory capacity \(\dim(\mathcal{H}_M)\) and free-energy budget \(\dot{W}\), its sensory-update and actuator channels implement an adaptive feedback loop that:

  1. Compresses environmental histories into an internal model \(\sigma_t\) with high predictive mutual information \(I(M_A : O_{\text{future}})\) relative to memory size;
  2. Updates \(\sigma_t\) in response to new observations \(o_t\) (learning/inference); and
  3. Selects actions \(a_t\) that achieve high expected objective performance \(\mathbb{E}[R]\) across \(\mathcal{E}\) per unit of thermodynamic free energy dissipated.

Look at what this definition accomplishes. First, it is completely third-person and operational: any external physicist can, in principle, measure \(A\)’s sensory-motor channels, memory dimension, energy dissipation, and task performance. Second, it is graded, not binary: a bacterium chemotaxing up a sugar gradient, a reinforcement-learning agent, and a human scientist sit on a well-defined physical spectrum of model depth, generalization breadth, and thermodynamic efficiency. Third, it makes no appeal to consciousness—an agent can satisfy this definition with zero subjective experience, a distinction we will sharpen in Chapter 7.

Worked check 4.3. Under this definition, why is a thermostat less intelligent than an adaptive predictive HVAC controller, even though both regulate room temperature?

Reasoned solution

A simple bimetallic thermostat stores only a 1-bit threshold error and cannot learn building thermal inertia, weather patterns, or occupancy schedules. The adaptive controller compresses multi-variable history into a predictive model to achieve tighter regulation with lower energy cost.
