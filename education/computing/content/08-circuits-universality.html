8.1

Circuit Identities

Circuit identities let us simplify gates and change the basis in which they act. We use them to construct phase markings and the reflection needed for search.

8.1.1 Rewriting circuits§

A circuit identity, such as \(HZH=X\), lets us simplify a computation or use different gates. By linearity, it suffices to check that the output amplitudes agree on every computational-basis input.

Start with cancellation. Since \(X\), \(Z\), \(H\), and CNOT are their own inverses, adjacent identical gates cancel. Gates on disjoint wires can be exchanged to bring a cancelling pair together. On the same wire, order matters: \(ZX=-XZ\), so \(ZXZ=-X\). This overall sign changes no measurement probability, but it must be retained if we later control the circuit: it then affects only the control-\(1\) branch.

Using \(HIH=I\) and \(HXH=Z\) on the two control branches gives controlled-\(Z\):

\[\mathrm{CZ}=(I\otimes H)\mathrm{CNOT}(I\otimes H),\qquad \mathrm{CZ}\ket a\ket b=(-1)^{ab}\ket a\ket b.\]

Since \(ab=ba\), \(\mathrm{CZ}\) is symmetric in its qubits. Conjugating \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to 2}\) by Hadamards on both wires therefore reverses its control and target:

\[\begin{aligned} (H\otimes H)\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to 2}\,(H\otimes H) &= (H\otimes H)(I\otimes H)\,\mathrm{CZ}\,(I\otimes H)(H\otimes H) \\ &= (H\otimes I)\,\mathrm{CZ}\,(H\otimes I) \\ &= \mathrm{CNOT}_{2\to 1}. \end{aligned}\]

For moving gates through controlled gates, with the first qubit as control and the rightmost operator acting first,

\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{CNOT}(X\otimes I)&=(X\otimes X)\mathrm{CNOT},\\ \mathrm{CNOT}(I\otimes Z)&=(Z\otimes Z)\mathrm{CNOT},\\ \mathrm{CZ}(X\otimes I)&=(X\otimes Z)\mathrm{CZ}. \end{aligned}\]

These follow directly from the basis action. An \(X\) on the CNOT target or a \(Z\) on its control commutes with CNOT; the CZ rule also holds with the qubits exchanged.

More generally, conjugation transfers a gate's action to a new basis: \[(VUV^\dagger)V\ket\psi=VU\ket\psi.\] Next we use it to turn a reflection about one direction into a reflection about another.

8.1.2 Reflections§

Linear algebra toolkit for reflections (click to expand)
  • Unitary, Hermitian, and self-inverse operators: A unitary operator satisfies \(U^\dagger U = I\). If it is also self-inverse (\(U^2 = I\)), multiplying by \(U^\dagger\) gives \(U = U^\dagger\) (Hermitian), and any eigenvalue \(\lambda\) satisfies \(\lambda^2 = 1\), so \(\lambda \in \{+1,-1\}\).
  • Orthogonal projectors: An operator \(P\) is an orthogonal projector if \(P^2 = P\) and \(P^\dagger = P\). For \(U^2 = I\), the operators \(P_+ = \tfrac{I+U}{2}\) and \(P_- = \tfrac{I-U}{2}\) are orthogonal projectors onto the \(+1\) and \(-1\) eigenspaces, satisfying \(P_+ + P_- = I\), \(P_+P_- = 0\), and \(U = P_+ - P_- = 2P_+ - I\).
  • Outer products (\(\ketbra{u}{v}\)): Unlike the inner product \(\braket{u}{v}\) (a scalar, row times column), the outer product \(\ketbra{u}{v}\) is a matrix (column times row) whose \((j,k)\) entry is \(u_j v_k^*\). It acts on any vector \(\ket{\psi}\) by \((\ketbra{u}{v})\ket{\psi} = \braket{v}{\psi}\ket{u}\), and obeys \((\ketbra{u}{v})^\dagger = \ketbra{v}{u}\). For example, \(\ketbra{0}{0} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\ketbra{1}{1} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\), and every entry of \(\ketbra{s}{s}\) is \(1/N\). When \(\ket{u}\) is a unit vector (\(\braket{u}{u}=1\)), \((\ketbra{u}{u})^2 = \ket{u}\braket{u}{u}\bra{u} = \ketbra{u}{u}\), so \(\ketbra{u}{u}\) is the rank-one orthogonal projector onto \(\ket{u}\) and \(2\ketbra{u}{u}-I\) keeps \(\ket{u}\) while negating every \(\ket{u_\perp}\).
  • Invariant subspaces and conjugation: A subspace \(W\) is invariant under \(U\) if \(U\ket{w}\in W\) for every \(\ket{w}\in W\) (e.g. \(\operatorname{span}\{\ket{s},\ket{0^n}\}\) under \(H^{\otimes n}\)). Conjugating by a unitary \(A\) maps \(\ketbra{u}{u}\) to \(A\ketbra{u}{u}A^\dagger = \ketbra{Au}{Au}\).
  • Angles and norms from inner products: For real unit vectors \(\ket{u},\ket{v}\), \(\braket{u}{v} = \cos\alpha\) gives the angle \(\alpha\) between them, and \(\|\ket{s}+\ket{0^n}\|^2 = \braket{s}{s}+\braket{0^n}{0^n}+2\braket{s}{0^n} = 2(1+2^{-n/2})\).

A unitary operator \(U\) is a reflection whenever it is self-inverse, \(U^2=I\). Because \(U^\dagger U=I\), self-inverseness forces \(U=U^\dagger\) (Hermitian) with eigenvalues \(\pm 1\). Every state splits orthogonally into \(+1\) and \(-1\) parts via \(\ket{\psi}=\tfrac{I+U}{2}\ket{\psi}+\tfrac{I-U}{2}\ket{\psi}\), so \(U\) keeps its \(+1\) eigenspace fixed and negates its orthogonal \(-1\) eigenspace. Writing \(P_+\) for the orthogonal projector onto the \(+1\) eigenspace (and \(P_-=I-P_+\)), every reflection unitary has the canonical form

\[U = P_+ - P_- = 2P_+ - I = I - 2P_-.\]

When the \(+1\) eigenspace is a single ray \(\ket{u}\) (\(P_+=\ketbra{u}{u}\)), the reflection about \(\ket{u}\) takes the rank-one form \(R_u = 2\ketbra{u}{u}-I\); its negative, \(-R_u = I - 2\ketbra{u}{u}\), flips the sign of \(\ket{u}\) alone while leaving every orthogonal state untouched. On any two-dimensional invariant plane where \(U\ne\pm I\), the \(+1\) eigenspace is a single line spanned by

\[\ket{u_+} = \frac{(I+U)\ket{\psi}}{\|(I+U)\ket{\psi}\|}\]

for any \(\ket{\psi}\) in the plane with \(U\ket{\psi}\ne-\ket{\psi}\), so \(U=2\ketbra{u_+}{u_+}-I\) on that plane.

Conjugation by any unitary \(A\) moves the mirror of a reflection from \(\ket{u}\) to \(A\ket{u}\):

\[A(2\ketbra{u}{u}-I)A^\dagger = 2\ketbra{Au}{Au}-I.\]

In particular, start from the reflection about \(\ket{0^n}\), \(R_0 = 2\ketbra{0^n}{0^n}-I = -O_{0^n}\), and conjugate by \(A=H^{\otimes n}\). Since \(H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n}=\ket{s}=2^{-n/2}\sum_x\ket{x}\), conjugation turns \(R_0\) into the reflection about the uniform state \(\ket{s}\):

\[D = H^{\otimes n}R_0H^{\otimes n} = 2\ketbra{s}{s}-I.\]

The conjugating layer \(H^{\otimes n}\) is itself a reflection because \((H^{\otimes n})^2=I\). Since \(H^{\otimes n}\) swaps \(\ket{0^n}\) and \(\ket{s}\), the plane \(\operatorname{span}\{\ket{s},\ket{0^n}\}\) is invariant. Applying the \(\ket{u_+}\) formula to \(\ket{\psi}=\ket{0^n}\) gives the reflection vector—their normalized angle bisector—and its orthogonal \(-1\) partner:

\[\ket{b_+} = \frac{\ket{s}+\ket{0^n}}{\sqrt{2(1+2^{-n/2})}},\qquad \ket{b_-} = \frac{\ket{0^n}-\ket{s}}{\sqrt{2(1-2^{-n/2})}},\]

with \(H^{\otimes n}\ket{b_+}=+\ket{b_+}\) and \(H^{\otimes n}\ket{b_-}=-\ket{b_-}\).

Since \(\braket{s}{0^n}=2^{-n/2}\), the angle between \(\ket{s}\) and \(\ket{0^n}\) is \(\alpha=\arccos(2^{-n/2})\) (for \(n=2\), \(\alpha=60^\circ\)). The bisector \(\ket{b_+}\) therefore lies at \(\alpha/2\) (\(30^\circ\) for \(n=2\)), and \(H^{\otimes n}\) acts on this plane as a mirror reflection across \(\ket{b_+}\), mapping any angle \(\theta\) to \(\alpha-\theta\). Conjugating \(R_0\) by \(H^{\otimes n}\) composes three reflections—across \(\alpha/2\), then \(\alpha\), then \(\alpha/2\) again: \(\theta \xrightarrow{H^{\otimes n}} \alpha-\theta \xrightarrow{R_0} \alpha+\theta \xrightarrow{H^{\otimes n}} -\theta\)—which moves the mirror from \(\ket{0^n}\) to \(\ket{s}\).

To picture the net reflection \(D=2\ketbra{s}{s}-I\) on any state, split \(\ket{\psi}\) into a part along \(\ket{s}\) and a part perpendicular to it,

\[\ket\psi=a\ket s+b\ket{s_\perp},\qquad a=\braket s\psi,\]

and apply \(D\):

\[D\ket\psi=a\ket s-b\ket{s_\perp}.\]

The component along \(\ket s\) passes through unchanged, and the perpendicular component reverses. That is a mirror in state space: a state at angle \(\theta\) from \(\ket s\) lands at \(-\theta\), and the component along \(\ket s\) is the only part that escapes the sign change.

Animated geometry: \(D = H^{\otimes 2} R_0 H^{\otimes 2}\)

For \(N=2^n\), the entries of the matrix take only two values:

\[\bra xD\ket y=\begin{cases}2/N-1,&x=y,\\2/N,&x\ne y.\end{cases}\]

Read as amplitudes, the rule is the same for every basis state: \(a_x\mapsto2\mu-a_x\), where \(\mu=N^{-1}\sum_y a_y\) is the mean amplitude. Each amplitude is reflected through the mean: whatever it exceeded the mean by, it now falls short by. Hence the name reflection about the mean.

Despite coupling every pair of distinct basis strings, the circuit needs only \(2n\) Hadamards, \(2n\) bit flips, and one multi-controlled \(Z\). With clean workspace, its gate count grows linearly in \(n\). Paired with a phase oracle, this reflection drives quantum search.

The marking circuit implements \(O_{0^n}=-R_0\), so the resulting circuit implements \(-D\). This global sign matters if the reflection is controlled. More generally, any preparation \(A\ket{0^n}=\ket\psi\) gives

\[AR_0A^\dagger=2\ketbra\psi\psi-I.\]

8.2

Controlled Gates

We begin by defining what a controlled gate is in general, then derive from first principles how to build the controlled version of any single-qubit unitary out of CNOT and one-qubit gates.

8.2.1 What is a controlled gate?§

Linear algebra & parity toolkit for controlled gates (click to expand)
  • Controlled-gate block form: A controlled-\(U\) gate acts as \(CU = \ketbra{0}{0}\otimes I + \ketbra{1}{1}\otimes U\), which is the block-diagonal matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(I, U)\).
  • Boolean parity-to-product identities: For bits \(a,b,c\in\{0,1\}\), \(a\oplus b = a+b-2ab\) gives \(ab = \tfrac12\bigl(a+b-(a\oplus b)\bigr)\), and for three bits \(4abc = a+b+c-(a\oplus b)-(a\oplus c)-(b\oplus c)+(a\oplus b\oplus c)\).
  • Spectral theorem for \(2\times 2\) unitaries: Every one-qubit unitary \(U\) has orthonormal eigenvectors \(\ket{e_0},\ket{e_1}\) with unit-modulus eigenvalues \(e^{i\alpha}, e^{i\beta}\), so \(U = W\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\alpha}, e^{i\beta})W^\dagger\) where \(W\ket{0}=\ket{e_0}\) and \(W\ket{1}=\ket{e_1}\).
  • Why global phase matters under control: Replacing \(U\) by \(e^{i\gamma}U\) turns \(CU\) into \(\ketbra{0}{0}\otimes I + e^{i\gamma}\ketbra{1}{1}\otimes U = (R_\gamma\otimes I)\,CU\), adding \(R_\gamma\) on the control wire.
  • Unitary square roots: Halving the eigenphases of \(U = W\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\alpha},e^{i\beta})W^\dagger\) defines \(V = W\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\alpha/2},e^{i\beta/2})W^\dagger\), which satisfies \(V^2 = U\).

Given any \(k\)-qubit unitary \(U\), its controlled version (\(\text{controlled-}U\), or \(CU\)) acts on a control qubit and a \(k\)-qubit target register: it leaves the target untouched when the control is \(\ket{0}\), and applies \(U\) when the control is \(\ket{1}\):

\[CU(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi}) = \ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi},\qquad CU(\ket{1}\otimes\ket{\psi}) = \ket{1}\otimes U\ket{\psi}.\]

Extending by linearity to any superposition \(\ket{c} = c_0\ket{0}+c_1\ket{1}\) on the control, the operator takes the orthogonal block form

\[CU = \ketbra{0}{0}\otimes I + \ketbra{1}{1}\otimes U = \begin{pmatrix}I & 0\\ 0 & U\end{pmatrix}.\]

Because \(I\) and \(U\) are both unitary and act on orthogonal control subspaces, \(CU\) is always unitary: \((CU)^\dagger(CU) = \ketbra{0}{0}\otimes I + \ketbra{1}{1}\otimes U^\dagger U = I\).

Notice one crucial consequence of this block structure: a gate's global phase is no longer global once it is controlled. Replacing \(U\) by \(e^{i\gamma}U\) multiplies only the \(\ket{1}\)-block by \(e^{i\gamma}\), turning \(CU\) into \((R_\gamma\otimes I)\,CU\), where \(R_\gamma = \operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\gamma})\) acts on the control wire. For example, controlled-\(X\) is CNOT, and controlled-\(Z\) is CZ—both already familiar. Our task now is to build \(\text{controlled-}U\) for an arbitrary single-qubit unitary \(U\) using only CNOT and one-qubit gates.

8.2.2 Controlled one-qubit gates from first principles§

To construct \(\text{controlled-}U\) for any one-qubit unitary \(U\) from first principles, we proceed in three steps: first build controlled phase gates from CNOT, then diagonalize \(U\) in its eigenbasis, and finally conjugate the controlled phase into \(U\).

Step 1: Controlled phase gates from parity. We want the gate

\[\text{controlled-}R_\phi\ket{a}\ket{b} = e^{i\phi\,ab}\ket{a}\ket{b},\qquad a,b\in\{0,1\},\]

using only CNOT and single-qubit phase gates \(R_\theta\ket{x} = e^{i\theta x}\ket{x}\). Writing the product \(ab\) in terms of addition and parity,

\[ab = \tfrac12\bigl(a+b-(a\oplus b)\bigr),\]

gives the four-gate sequence on \(\ket{a}\ket{b}\) step by step:

\[\begin{aligned} \ket{a}\ket{b} &\;\xrightarrow{\ R_{\phi/2}\otimes R_{\phi/2}\ }\; e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}(a+b)}\ket{a}\ket{b} \\ &\;\xrightarrow{\quad\text{CNOT}_{1\to 2}\quad}\; e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}(a+b)}\ket{a}\ket{a\oplus b} \\ &\;\xrightarrow{\quad I\otimes R_{-\phi/2}\quad}\; e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}(a+b-(a\oplus b))}\ket{a}\ket{a\oplus b} \\ &\;\xrightarrow{\quad\text{CNOT}_{1\to 2}\quad}\; e^{i\phi\,ab}\ket{a}\ket{b}. \end{aligned}\]

Two standard phase gates are \(S=R_{\pi/2}=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)\) and \(T=R_{\pi/4}=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\pi/4})\), with \(T^2=S\) and \(S^2=Z\); CZ uses \(S,S,S^\dagger\) and controlled-\(S\) uses \(T,T,T^\dagger\), with two CNOTs in each case.

Step 2: Diagonalize \(U\). Every one-qubit unitary \(U\) has orthonormal eigenvectors \(\ket{e_0},\ket{e_1}\) with unit-modulus eigenvalues \(e^{i\alpha},e^{i\beta}\):

\[U\ket{e_0}=e^{i\alpha}\ket{e_0},\qquad U\ket{e_1}=e^{i\beta}\ket{e_1}.\]

Choosing the unitary \(W\) that maps \(\ket{0}\mapsto\ket{e_0}\) and \(\ket{1}\mapsto\ket{e_1}\) diagonalizes \(U\):

\[U=W\Lambda W^\dagger,\qquad\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\alpha},e^{i\beta})=e^{i\alpha}R_{\beta-\alpha}.\]

Step 3: Assemble controlled-\(U\). Conjugating the target by \(W\) moves \(\Lambda = e^{i\alpha}R_{\beta-\alpha}\) into the eigenbasis of \(U\):

\[\text{controlled-}U=(I\otimes W)\,\bigl(\text{controlled-}R_{\beta-\alpha}\bigr)\,(R_\alpha\otimes I)\,(I\otimes W^\dagger).\]

Checking each control value equation by equation:

\[\begin{aligned} \ket{0}\ket{t} &\;\xrightarrow{\ I\otimes W^\dagger\ }\; \ket{0}(W^\dagger\ket{t}) \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{controlled-}R_{\beta-\alpha}\,(R_\alpha\otimes I)\ }\; \ket{0}(W^\dagger\ket{t}) \;\xrightarrow{\ I\otimes W\ }\; \ket{0}\ket{t}, \\ \ket{1}\ket{t} &\;\xrightarrow{\ I\otimes W^\dagger\ }\; \ket{1}(W^\dagger\ket{t}) \;\xrightarrow{\ R_\alpha\otimes I\ }\; e^{i\alpha}\ket{1}(W^\dagger\ket{t}) \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{controlled-}R_{\beta-\alpha}\ }\; e^{i\alpha}\ket{1}(R_{\beta-\alpha}W^\dagger\ket{t}) \;\xrightarrow{\ I\otimes W\ }\; \ket{1}(W\Lambda W^\dagger\ket{t}) = \ket{1}U\ket{t}. \end{aligned}\]

The entire construction costs two CNOTs and one-qubit gates. For instance, taking \(U=X\) gives \(W=H\), \(\alpha=0\), \(\beta=\pi\), recovering \(\text{CNOT}=(I\otimes H)\,\text{CZ}\,(I\otimes H)\).

8.2.3 Two controls from one§

The same parity identity builds a gate with two controls. Choose a unitary square root \(V^2=U\), obtained by halving the eigenphases of \(U\). For control bits \(a,b\in\{0,1\}\),

\[V^{\,b-(a\oplus b)+a}=V^{2ab}=U^{ab}.\]

Using five two-qubit gates—three singly controlled gates and two CNOTs between the controls—gives

\[CCU = (C_a V)\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{a\to b}\,(C_b V^\dagger)\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{a\to b}\,(C_b V),\]

which acts on \(\ket{a}\ket{b}\ket{t}\) step by step as

\[\begin{aligned} \ket{a}\ket{b}\ket{t} &\;\xrightarrow{\ C_b V\ }\; \ket{a}\ket{b}V^b\ket{t} \;\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{CNOT}_{a\to b}\ }\; \ket{a}\ket{a\oplus b}V^b\ket{t} \\ &\;\xrightarrow{\ C_b V^\dagger\ }\; \ket{a}\ket{a\oplus b}V^{b-(a\oplus b)}\ket{t} \;\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{CNOT}_{a\to b}\ }\; \ket{a}\ket{b}V^{b-(a\oplus b)}\ket{t} \;\xrightarrow{\ C_a V\ }\; \ket{a}\ket{b}U^{ab}\ket{t}. \end{aligned}\]

Setting \(U=Z\) and \(V=S\) gives doubly controlled-\(Z\) (\(CCZ\)), and conjugating the target by \(H\) gives Toffoli: \(\text{Toffoli} = (I\otimes I\otimes H)\,CCZ\,(I\otimes I\otimes H)\). Decomposing each controlled-\(S\) or controlled-\(S^\dagger\) as above uses eight CNOTs, nine \(T\)-type gates, and two Hadamards (or six CNOTs and seven \(T\)-type gates in an optimized layout).

For \(n\) controls, compute their AND into clean workspace, apply controlled-\(U\), and uncompute. The Toffoli construction therefore reduces every multi-controlled one-qubit gate to CNOT and one-qubit gates, with gate count proportional to \(n\) when sufficient clean workspace is available.

8.3

Universal Gate Sets

To build an arbitrary unitary, we first break it into gates that mix two basis strings at a time. This establishes universality; counting parameters then reveals its cost.

8.3.1 Two-level unitaries§

To assemble an arbitrary unitary on \(N=2^n\) basis strings, first reduce it to two-level unitaries: gates that mix two basis amplitudes by a \(2\times2\) unitary and fix all others. For example, \(H\otimes I\) is a product of two such gates, acting on the pairs \(00,10\) and \(01,11\).

Clear the first column of \(U\) one entry at a time. For its top entry \(p\) and a lower entry \(q\), set \(r=\sqrt{|p|^2+|q|^2}\). When \(r>0\),

\[G=\frac1r\begin{pmatrix}\bar p&\bar q\\-q&p\end{pmatrix},\qquad G\begin{pmatrix}p\\q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r\\0\end{pmatrix}.\]

Skipping pairs with \(r=0\) and applying \(G_{1,k}\) to rows \(1\) and \(k\) for \(k=2,\dots,N\) clears the first column; row normalization then forces the rest of the first row to vanish, leaving an \((N-1)\times(N-1)\) unitary \(U'\):

\[G_{1,N}\cdots G_{1,2}U = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & U'\end{pmatrix}.\]

Repeating on \(U'\) (at most \(N(N-1)/2\) steps) leaves only a phase on \(\ket{N-1}\), which a diagonal two-level gate removes; inverting the product expresses \(U\) as a product of two-level unitaries.

8.3.2 Universality of CNOT and one-qubit gates§

If the strings \(a,b\) mixed by a two-level unitary differ only at bit \(j\), apply its \(2\times2\) gate \(\tilde U\) on wire \(j\), controlled by their shared bits. Surround each control requiring \(0\) with \(X\) gates, as in the marking construction.

For strings differing in several bits, flip those bits one at a time to form a Gray code:

\[a=g_0,g_1,\ldots,g_m=b,\qquad m\le n.\]

Each neighbouring pair differs in one bit, so a multi-controlled \(X\) swap \(S_{g_k,g_{k-1}}\) exchanges their amplitudes. Conjugating \(\tilde{U}\) on \((g_0,g_1)\) by \(m-1\) swaps builds the two-level unitary on \((a,b)\) using \(2m-1 < 2n\) multi-controlled gates:

\[U_{a,b} = S_{g_m,g_{m-1}}\cdots S_{g_2,g_1}\,\tilde{U}_{g_0,g_1}\,S_{g_2,g_1}\cdots S_{g_m,g_{m-1}}.\]

Every unitary reduces to two-level gates, each of these to multi-controlled gates, and each controlled gate to CNOT and one-qubit gates. Thus CNOT together with all one-qubit gates is exactly universal: it can build every unitary on a finite qubit register.

Both resources are essential. CNOT alone cannot create superpositions from basis states; one-qubit gates alone cannot entangle. Exact universality here assumes arbitrary one-qubit gates are available, including the basis changes used in their controlled constructions.

Further detail: Barenco et al.: Elementary gates for quantum computation.

8.3.3 The cost of a generic unitary§

The construction uses order \(4^n\) two-level gates, each requiring order \(n^2\) elementary gates: order \(n^2 4^n\) gates overall. A parameter count shows that the exponential growth is unavoidable for most unitaries.

An \(N\times N\) complex matrix has \(2N^2\) real parameters. Column normalization imposes \(N\) real conditions, and orthogonality imposes two per pair of columns. Removing global phase leaves

\[2N^2-N-N(N-1)-1=N^2-1=4^n-1.\]

Each one-qubit gate contributes at most three parameters up to global phase; CNOT contributes none. A fixed arrangement of \(L\) gates therefore has at most \(3L\) continuous parameters. There are only finitely many arrangements at each length.

Assumption. A smooth family with fewer independent parameters cannot fill a higher-dimensional region of unitaries. Consequently, almost every unitary requires

\[L\ge\frac{4^n-1}{3}.\]

This does not prove that any particular unitary is expensive. Structured operations, such as a single-string phase oracle or the uniform-state reflection, can have short circuits despite acting on exponentially many amplitudes.

8.3.4 Finite gate sets and approximation§

A finite gate set has only finitely many circuits at each length, hence countably many circuits in total. The continuously varying phase gates \(R_\theta\) already form an uncountable family. Exact universality must therefore give way to approximation.

The operator-norm error measures the largest output-state error:

\[\|U-V\|=\max_{\|\psi\|=1}\bigl\|(U-V)\ket\psi\bigr\|.\]

If this is at most \(\epsilon\), each basis-outcome probability changes by at most \(2\epsilon\). For \(u=\braket{y}{U\psi}\) and \(v=\braket{y}{V\psi}\),

\[\bigl||u|^2-|v|^2\bigr|=\bigl||u|-|v|\bigr|\bigl(|u|+|v|\bigr)\le2|u-v|\le2\epsilon.\]

Errors accumulate at most linearly, because

\[V_2V_1-U_2U_1=V_2(V_1-U_1)+(V_2-U_2)U_1\]

and unitary factors preserve the norm. By induction, accuracy \(\epsilon/L\) per gate suffices for total error \(\epsilon\) over \(L\) gates. Match global phases before comparing gates, unless they are controlled.

The set \(\{H,S,\mathrm{CNOT},T\}\) is called Clifford+T; \(S=T^2\) and \(T^\dagger=T^7\). Without \(T\), only finitely many one-qubit gates are reachable up to phase. With basis-state inputs and computational-basis measurements, these Clifford circuits can be simulated efficiently classically. Adding \(T\) allows arbitrary one-qubit gates to be approximated.

Theorem (Solovay–Kitaev, quoted). Up to global phase, every one-qubit gate can be approximated to operator-norm error \(\delta\) by a sequence of \(H\) and \(T\) gates of length proportional to \(\log^c(1/\delta)\), for a fixed constant \(c\).

Thus a circuit of \(L\) CNOTs and arbitrary one-qubit gates needs order \(L\log^c(L/\epsilon)\) Clifford+T gates for accuracy \(\epsilon\), up to an overall phase. Controlled gates must first be decomposed with their relative phases intact. The finite gate set adds a slowly growing overhead to the original circuit cost.

8.4

Physical Constraints

Circuit synthesis assumes ideal gates and unrestricted connections. Physical devices impose routing costs and noise, motivating state transport and error correction.

8.4.1 Connectivity and SWAP§

A device's connectivity specifies which qubits can interact directly. To apply a gate between uncoupled qubits, move their states using the SWAP gate, \(\mathrm{SWAP} = \mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to 2}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{2\to 1}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to 2}\):

\[\ket a\ket b\longrightarrow\ket a\ket{a\oplus b}\longrightarrow\ket b\ket{a\oplus b}\longrightarrow\ket b\ket a.\]

By linearity, this also exchanges qubits in superpositions or entangled states. On a line, qubits separated by \(d\) edges become neighbours after \(d-1\) swaps, costing \(3(d-1)\) extra CNOTs. Restoring their positions doubles that overhead; a compiler can instead track their new locations. Routing adds gate count and depth without changing the intended computation.

8.4.2 Correcting bit flips§

The preceding error bound requires increasingly accurate gates as a circuit grows. Error correction takes another approach: encode a state so that errors can be detected without measuring its amplitudes.

Encoding with two CNOTs maps a data qubit and two blank qubits to \(\ket{\psi_L}\):

\[\mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to 3}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to 2}\,(a\ket0+b\ket1)\ket0\ket0 = a\ket{000}+b\ket{111} = \ket{\psi_L}.\]

This distributes one qubit of information across three; it does not clone the state. If the second qubit flips, \(X_2\ket{\psi_L} = a\ket{010}+b\ket{101}\), both branches share the same neighbouring parities, so measuring those parities detects the error without distinguishing \(a\) from \(b\).

Using four CNOTs into two ancillas extracts the syndrome \((x_1\oplus x_2,\, x_2\oplus x_3)\):

\[\ket{x_1 x_2 x_3}\ket{0}\ket{0} \;\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to 4}\mathrm{CNOT}_{2\to 4}\mathrm{CNOT}_{2\to 5}\mathrm{CNOT}_{3\to 5}\ }\; \ket{x_1 x_2 x_3}\ket{x_1\oplus x_2}\ket{x_2\oplus x_3}.\]

Assuming at most one bit flip, the syndrome uniquely identifies the correction:

\[\begin{array}{c|cccc} \text{syndrome}&(0,0)&(1,0)&(1,1)&(0,1)\\ \text{correction}&I&X_1&X_2&X_3 \end{array}\]

Even a small rotation \(R_x(\theta)\) on the second qubit is correctable. It gives

\[\cos\tfrac\theta2\ket{\psi_L}-i\sin\tfrac\theta2X_2\ket{\psi_L}.\]

Syndrome measurement collapses this continuous error onto one of two discrete correction branches:

\[(0,0)\text{ with }p=\cos^2\tfrac\theta2 \;\longrightarrow\; \ket{\psi_L},\qquad (1,1)\text{ with }p=\sin^2\tfrac\theta2 \;\longrightarrow\; -iX_2\ket{\psi_L} \;\xrightarrow{\ X_2\ }\; -i\ket{\psi_L}.\]

Two flips instead give the syndrome of the remaining qubit, so correction completes a logical bit flip. A phase flip is invisible to these parity checks:

\[Z_1\ket{\psi_L}=a\ket{000}-b\ket{111}.\]

Protecting against both kinds of error, including errors during correction, requires larger codes. The principle remains: measure information about the error without revealing the encoded amplitudes.
